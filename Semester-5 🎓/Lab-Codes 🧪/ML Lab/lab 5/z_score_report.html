Finding Outliers using Z-Score Method in Python

1. Objective

The objective of this laboratory experiment is to detect and eliminate anomalous observations from a dataset using the Z-Score method, which quantifies how many standard deviations an observation deviates from the arithmetic mean. In this laboratory study, the method is deployed on an institutional placement dataset comprising 1,000 student records, and the effects of trimming outliers on distribution contours and data dispersion are evaluated using kernel density histograms and quartile boxplots.

2. Theory

Outliers are empirical data points that deviate markedly from the remaining observations within a distribution. They frequently stem from measurement or transcription errors, sensor faults, or authentic but low-probability phenomena. In either circumstance, unhandled extreme values can distort parametric summary measures—most notably the mean and standard deviation—and compromise the reliability of downstream predictive and inferential models.

The Z-score expresses the relative divergence of an observation from the mean normalized by the standard deviation:

Z = X − μ σ

Where:

  • X = individual observation value
  • μ = mean of the dataset
  • σ = standard deviation of the dataset

Under standard practice, an observation with |Z| > 3 is flagged as an outlier. This criterion assumes that the feature follows an approximately normal (Gaussian) distribution, where roughly 99.7% of all data points naturally fall within three standard deviations of the mean.

7.6 Note on the Standard Deviation Used

The notebook computes standard deviation using pandas .std(), which divides by n − 1 (sample formula). By contrast, scipy.stats.zscore employs population standard deviation (dividing by n). With n = 1,000, the resulting numerical difference is roughly 0.05%, producing identical outlier classifications under both approaches.

9. Conclusion

The Z-score method serves as an efficient, intuitive tool for screening anomalous data points in attributes conforming to a Gaussian distribution, as demonstrated by the CGPA feature. However, its primary constraint is the assumption of normality: applying Z-score thresholds to skewed variables produces false-positive detections along legitimate tails. For non-normal features, nonparametric strategies such as the IQR rule, median/MAD modified Z-scores, or Isolation Forests should be utilized. Furthermore, domain practitioners must review flagged points prior to deletion, since rare empirical observations often convey critical information rather than errors.

10. Questions

Q1. What is an outlier?

An outlier is an empirical data point that deviates markedly from the predominant pattern established by the remainder of the dataset. Such anomalies can stem from procedural faults, measurement errors, sensor malfunctions, or genuine extreme natural variations. In this laboratory study, CGPA values of 4.89 and 9.12 exemplify outliers: both reside more than 3 standard deviations away from the sample mean of 6.96.

Q2. How is Z-score calculated?

The Z-score of an observation is computed by subtracting the dataset mean from the observed value and dividing the result by the standard deviation: Z = (X − μ) / σ. The sign indicates directionality (positive for values above average, negative for values below), while the magnitude reflects the distance from the mean in standard deviation units. In pandas, this is vectorized as:

placement_df['cgpa_zscore'] = (placement_df['cgpa'] - placement_df['cgpa'].mean()) / placement_df['cgpa'].std()

For example, a student CGPA of 9.12 yields Z = (9.12 − 6.961) / 0.616 ≈ 3.51, indicating an observation located 3.51 standard deviations above the average.

Q3. Why do we take |Z| > 3 as a threshold?

This cutoff is derived directly from the Empirical Rule (68-95-99.7 rule) governing normal distributions: roughly 68% of data points fall within ±1σ, 95% within ±2σ, and 99.7% within ±3σ. Consequently, only ~0.27% of observations in a Gaussian distribution are expected to lie beyond ±3 standard deviations, making such events sufficiently rare to warrant scrutiny. A lower threshold such as 2 would flag ~4.6% of typical data, causing excessive false alarms, while a higher threshold would fail to isolate genuine anomalies.

The threshold of 3 represents a widely accepted convention balancing detection sensitivity against unwarranted data loss. In our experimental dataset, 5 of 1,000 records (0.5%) exceeded the threshold, closely matching the 0.27% expected under theoretical normality.

Q4. What are some limitations of the Z-score method?
  • Normality Assumption: The method requires that data be approximately bell-shaped. On skewed features such as placement_exam_marks, it flags genuine observations along the tail while overlooking anomalies on the truncated side.
  • Vulnerability of Baseline Parameters: The sample mean and standard deviation are non-robust statistics sensitive to the very outliers being detected. Extreme values inflate both parameters, masking secondary outliers.
  • Sample Size Constraints: In very small samples, the maximum mathematically possible Z-score is constrained, preventing genuine outliers from reaching the |Z| > 3 threshold.
  • Univariate Restriction: The method examines features individually and cannot identify multi-attribute anomalies that appear typical in one dimension but anomalous in combination.
  • Heuristic Cutoff: The threshold of 3 is a heuristic convention; an observation beyond 3 standard deviations is not automatically an error.
  • Nonparametric Alternatives: For skewed or multivariate data, the IQR method, modified Z-scores (median and MAD), or Isolation Forests provide far more reliable results.
Q5. How does removing outliers affect model accuracy in machine learning?

The impact of outlier filtering depends on algorithm sensitivity and the validity of the extreme observations:

  • Accuracy Improvements in Sensitive Models: Outlier trimming benefits algorithms sensitive to extreme magnitude deviations, such as linear and logistic regression, Support Vector Machines, KNN, and k-means clustering. Outliers exert disproportionate leverage on loss functions, pulling regression slopes and decision boundaries away from optimal convergence. Removing genuine anomalies stabilizes parameter estimates and boosts generalization.
  • Negligible Impact on Robust Models: Tree-based architectures (decision trees, Random Forests, Gradient Boosting) partition feature spaces via threshold splits and are largely invariant to monotonic outliers. Trimming 0.5% of rows causes negligible change in tree predictions.
  • Risk of Degrading Real-World Generalization: If outliers represent genuine, highly informative occurrences (e.g., exceptional students, rare medical events, fraud), deleting them deprives the model of essential domain variance. Removing excessive observations also contracts training volume.

Therefore, outliers should be investigated systematically, and candidate models should be benchmarked with and without outlier filtering using rigorous cross-validation to substantiate performance gains.
